# Chapter 11: Moving Forward

## Core Idea
Ten steps for building a culture of usability metrics: sell it, start small, budget realistically, plan ahead, benchmark, explore data, speak business, show confidence, don't misuse metrics, and simplify the presentation.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Ten steps**
  1. **Sell usability and the power of metrics**: bring decision makers to watch sessions (consistent patterns convince; one session is easily dismissed); show short clips (under 30 s) of 2–3 participants hitting the *same* problem; tie metrics to ROI; find an executive champion.
  2. **Start small and work your way up**: begin with issue categories, severity and SUS after each session; then efficiency (time, lostness), expectations and awareness gaps, levels of success; then combined scores and scorecards.
  3. **Make sure you have the time and money**: simple metrics added to qualitative studies cost up to about 10% more (estimates within ±25%); broad metric studies range from half to twice a lab test. Estimate preparation, collection and analysis separately. If short on time, release top-line results first and detail later.
  4. **Plan early and often**: "reverse engineer" the data by sketching the key slides first, then design the study to produce that data; run fake data through the analysis; pilot with 1–2 participants.
  5. **Benchmark your products**: metrics are relative. Benchmark effectiveness (success), efficiency (time) and satisfaction (ease ratings) consistently; even routine SUS gives a cross-project baseline; add competitors.
  6. **Explore your data**: clean it, derive variables (top-2-box, success totals, expert ratio, time bands), visualize several ways, bring in other sources; ask vendors for raw data.
  7. **Speak the language of business**: frame findings as lower costs or higher revenue; pick metrics tied to business goals (call-center deflection → completion and abandonment; e-commerce → checkout abandonment).
  8. **Show your confidence**: CIs always, especially for n < 20; p-values for comparisons; show small-sample success as "6 of 8"; avoid false precision.
  9. **Don't misuse metrics**: skip them when they add no value (early qualitative work, rapid iterations); don't present too much ("pack, then cut in half"); don't measure 100 things; don't rely on a single metric.
  10. **Simplify your presentation**: know the audience (the project team wants issues and fixes; sponsors want goals, timeline and budget; executives want the big picture through stories and clips).

## Key Concepts
- **Reverse engineering the data**: visualize the end charts before designing the study.
- **CI overlap rule**: non-overlapping CIs mean a significant difference; overlapping CIs can still be significant, so test.

## Mental Models
- The power of observation lies in *consistent patterns*, not single sessions.
- Every chart needs a story: why the task was hard, shown with metrics, verbatims and clips, plus a design solution.
- Give the audience 2–3 findings to latch onto.

## Anti-patterns
- **Showing one participant** (easily dismissed as an edge case) or one easy session (false security).
- **Describing data instead of telling its story.**
- **Collecting metrics nobody will act on.**

## Worked Example
Presentation checklist from the chapter: set the stage for the audience → keep procedural detail available but brief (participants, tasks) → **lead with positive findings** → annotated screenshots → short embedded video clips → **one summary-metrics slide** (task completion overview, comparison to goals, overall usability score or scorecard) → for executives, fewer metrics and more stories.

## Key Takeaways
1. Get stakeholders to watch real sessions and show multi-participant clips.
2. Start with issues + severity + SUS; grow the toolkit over time.
3. Sketch the final charts before designing the study.
4. Benchmark consistently on success, time and satisfaction.
5. Always show confidence; never show false precision.
6. Fewer, well-chosen metrics, told as a story, led by positives.
